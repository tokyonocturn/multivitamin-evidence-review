# Multivitamins: An Independent Scientific Review

**What they contain, what the trials show, who benefits, and what they cost**

Version 1.0 · November 2026

---

## Abstract

Multivitamin and multimineral (MVM) supplements are used by roughly one in three
US adults and generate tens of billions of dollars in annual sales. They are also
among the most heavily studied products in nutrition, with randomised trials
enrolling more than 100,000 participants and follow-up extending to 12 years.

The evidence is consistent and unflattering to the marketing:

- **Mortality:** no benefit. Nine pooled RCTs (n=51,550) give OR 0.94
  (0.87-1.01). A 390,124-person cohort found a 4% *higher* mortality risk among
  daily users (HR 1.04, 1.02-1.07), almost certainly confounded but incompatible
  with a large protective effect.
- **Cardiovascular disease:** no benefit. PHS II HR 1.02 (0.92-1.13); COSMOS HR
  0.98 (0.86-1.12); pooled CVD mortality OR 0.94 (0.83-1.06).
- **Cancer:** a small reduction in incidence (OR 0.93, 0.87-0.99), driven mainly by
  PHS II, with no reduction in cancer mortality (OR 0.94, 0.81-1.09). The absolute
  difference is about 2 to 12 fewer cancers per 1,000 people over 3 to 11 years.
- **Cognition:** a small but consistent benefit in older adults in three COSMOS
  substudies (about 0.07 SD, roughly 2 years less cognitive aging), absent in the
  12-year PHS II cognitive substudy. The most interesting recent finding, and not
  yet replicated outside one trial.
- **Immunity and energy:** no reliable benefit in well-nourished adults. The
  "energy" claim is a description of B-vitamin biochemistry, not a clinical effect.
- **Pregnancy:** folic acid reduces neural tube defects by about 72%. This is the
  strongest evidence in the field and the one indication where a multivitamin is
  unambiguously standard care.

The category is also poorly regulated relative to its scale. Independent testing
finds failures in roughly one quarter to one third of products: sub-potent vitamin
A, four-fold overages of folate or zinc, tablets that do not dissolve. Twenty
products sampled here range from **$0.05 to $3.30 per daily serving — a 70-fold
spread** — with no relationship between price and evidence.

The conclusion is not that multivitamins are useless. It is that **the average
benefit is small, the subgroups that benefit are defined by nutritional need rather
than by brand or format, and the money is better spent on the specific nutrients a
person actually lacks.** Pregnancy, documented deficiency, bariatric surgery,
malabsorption, vegan diets and poor dietary intake are the situations where the
case is strong. For a healthy, well-nourished adult, the expected benefit is close
to zero and the expected cost is $20-$1,200 per year.

## 1. Scope and definitions

### 1.1 What counts as a multivitamin

There is no legal definition. The term covers products containing roughly 10 to 30
vitamins and minerals, from a $0.05 tablet with 100% of the Daily Value of 23
nutrients to a $3.30 greens powder with 7 g of unquantified plant blends. The
United States Pharmacopeia and the NIH both note that "multivitamin" is a marketing
and convenience category, not a chemically defined one [R57].

This review includes products that declare at least five vitamins and/or minerals
and are marketed as general nutrition. It excludes single nutrients, sports-only
products, herbal blends without declared vitamins, and prescription products.

### 1.2 What this review is not

- Not medical advice. People with kidney, liver or thyroid disease, haemochromatosis,
  who take warfarin, levothyroxine, methotrexate or retinoids, who are pregnant, or
  who have unexplained symptoms should talk to a clinician.
- Not a primary laboratory study. Quality conclusions come from published
  independent testing and certifier databases.
- Not a census of the market. Twenty products were selected purposively to cover
  formats and price tiers.
- Not free of uncertainty. Supplement research is partly industry-funded,
  publication bias is real, and the trials tested a narrow slice of the market.

## 2. The product category: regulation, labels and architecture

### 2.1 Regulation

In the United States, dietary supplements are regulated as a food category under
the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994. The FDA does not approve
them for safety or efficacy before sale. Manufacturers must follow current Good
Manufacturing Practice (21 CFR Part 111) [R55], and labels must be truthful and
not misleading, but there is no pre-market review of whether a product works.

This has two consequences. First, "FDA registered facility" means the building is
registered, not that the product is approved. Second, the quality of what is in the
bottle depends on the manufacturer and, where it exists, third-party testing.

### 2.2 What the label tells you

A US Supplement Facts panel gives the amount per serving and the percent Daily
Value (%DV). The %DV is based on the 2020 Daily Values, which are reference intakes
for the general population, not personalised targets. Three limitations matter:

1. **Serving size is elastic.** Two capsules, three gummies or a scoop can all be
   "one serving". A product that looks cheap per bottle can be expensive per day.
2. **Forms are often undisclosed.** "Vitamin D 25 mcg" may be D3 or D2; "folate 667
   mcg DFE" may be folic acid or 5-MTHF; "iron 18 mg" may be ferrous sulfate or
   bisglycinate. The form affects absorption and tolerability.
3. **Blends hide doses.** "Immune support blend 500 mg" with 12 ingredients tells
   you nothing about how much of each. Unquantified blends cannot be evaluated.

### 2.3 The architecture of the category

Products differ less than the shelf suggests. The main axes are:

| Axis | What changes | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target group | Iron, calcium, vitamin D, zinc doses | Iron is removed from men's and 50+ formulas; women's formulas add it |
| Age | 50+ formulas add B12 and vitamin D, remove iron | Lower acid and lower intake with age |
| Format | Tablet, capsule, gummy, powder | Determines dose ceiling, stability, sugar, cost |
| Source | Synthetic, food-based, "whole food" | Price and marketing more than absorption |
| Potency | 100% DV versus 500-3,000% DV | Drives UL exposure and side effects |
| Extras | K2, CoQ10, lutein, probiotics, blends | Price, unproven benefit, interaction risk |

The "men's" and "women's" distinction is mostly the iron decision plus a few
cosmetic dose changes. The "50+" distinction is mostly iron removal plus more B12
and D. Neither is a fundamentally different product.

## 3. The market: who takes them and why

About one in three US adults takes a multivitamin [R09]. The most common stated
reason is "to improve overall health" or "to fill nutrient gaps", not a diagnosed
deficiency [R58]. Use is higher among older adults, women, people with higher
education and income, and people who already eat well — the group with the least to
gain [R59].

This is the healthy-user effect, and it explains a lot of the observational
literature. People who take multivitamins also smoke less, exercise more, eat more
vegetables and see doctors more often. When a cohort finds that supplement users
live longer, it is usually measuring the person, not the pill.

The market is also segmented by anxiety. "Energy" formulas target fatigue; "immune"
formulas target colds; "hair, skin and nails" formulas target appearance; "greens"
powders target a vague sense of not eating enough vegetables. None of these
categories has outcome evidence to match its marketing.

## 4. Formulation analysis

### 4.1 Nutrient count versus dose

A tablet can hold only so much. Products with 25 or more nutrients tend to have
token amounts of the bulky minerals (calcium, magnesium) and low doses of the
expensive ones. Products with 9 nutrients can afford higher doses of each.

The trade-off is visible in the sample. Garden of Life's whole-food tablet declares
25 nutrients but supplies 2.5 mg iron, 3.9 mg zinc, no calcium and no magnesium,
because the organic food blend takes up mass. Ritual's women's formula declares
9 nutrients but supplies 50 mcg vitamin D, 1,000 mcg folate and 90 mcg K2.
Neither approach is inherently better; they answer different questions.

### 4.2 The iron decision

Iron is the most consequential formulation choice. The UL is 45 mg/day, and 18 mg
in a general multi is 40% of it. Most men and postmenopausal women do not need
supplemental iron, and in undiagnosed haemochromatosis it accumulates and causes
organ damage [R26].

A men's or 50+ formula without iron is a feature. A women's formula with 8-18 mg
is reasonable for menstruating women, but ferritin should guide the choice: a woman
with normal iron stores does not need 18 mg/day indefinitely.

### 4.3 The vitamin D decision

Vitamin D is the nutrient most often under-dosed. In the sample, Kirkland supplies
400 IU (10 mcg), the mass-market standard is 1,000 IU (25 mcg), and premium
products supply 2,000 IU (50 mcg). The difference matters: 400 IU will do little
for a person with a low 25(OH)D level.

Vitamin D status in the US is mixed. The CDC reports that insufficiency
(25(OH)D < 50 nmol/L) fell from 28% to 22% between 2001-2002 and 2021-2023, while
excess (> 125 nmol/L) rose from under 1% to 8%, mostly among supplement users
[R49]. The population is simultaneously under- and over-supplemented, which is what
you would expect from a category with no dosing logic.

### 4.4 The zinc-copper balance

Zinc induces metallothionein in the intestinal cells, which binds copper and
prevents its absorption. At 50 mg/day for weeks, this can cause copper deficiency,
anaemia and a myelopathy that mimics B12 deficiency [R24; R25]. The UL is 40 mg.

The design rule is simple: above 15 mg zinc, include about 1 mg copper per 15-25 mg
zinc. One A Day's 50+ formulas supply 24 mg zinc with 2.2 mg copper, which is
defensible. Pure Encapsulations O.N.E. supplies 25 mg zinc with no copper, which is
a design flaw even though the zinc dose is below the UL. Several "immune" products
sell 25-50 mg zinc with no copper at all.

### 4.5 B-vitamin potency

High-potency B vitamins are the main way a multivitamin exceeds a UL. Thorne Basic
Nutrients supplies 80 mg niacin (229% of the 35 mg UL) and 20 mg B6. Life Extension
supplies 50 mg niacin and 75 mg B6 (75% of the 100 mg UL). Opti-Men supplies 75 mg
niacin and 50 mg B6.

These doses are not accidental. They are there to produce a "high-potency" claim
and, in some cases, a noticeable flush that users interpret as evidence the product
is working. The clinical problem is that niacin above the UL causes flushing and,
at gram doses, hepatotoxicity, and B6 above 100 mg causes sensory neuropathy. A
person taking a high-potency multi plus a B-complex can reach those doses without
realising it.

### 4.6 Calcium and magnesium: token amounts

A multivitamin typically supplies 100-300 mg calcium and 20-100 mg magnesium,
against daily requirements of 1,000-1,300 mg and 320-420 mg. These are top-ups,
not treatments. Calcium carbonate also needs gastric acid, which is reduced in
older adults and by proton pump inhibitors; calcium citrate does not.

A person who needs calcium or magnesium should buy those separately, in the right
form and dose, rather than expecting a multi to cover it.

### 4.7 The whole-food premium

"Whole food", "food-grown", "cultured" and "food-based" are marketing categories,
not regulatory ones. The claim is better absorption and gentler digestion. The
evidence does not support a general advantage.

A 30-day single-blind randomised trial in 62 adults compared organically sourced
and synthetic multis. Circulating vitamin and mineral changes were comparable, with
no significant between-group differences [R63]. Smaller studies of fat-soluble
vitamins extracted from foods show comparable digestive recovery to synthetic forms.
Human steady-state studies of vitamin C from kiwifruit versus synthetic ascorbate
found no difference.

What whole-food products reliably deliver is a higher price and, often, a lower
mineral dose. That is a legitimate preference for some buyers, but it is not a
bioavailability advantage.

### 4.8 Gummies and powders

Gummies are the fastest-growing format and the weakest formulation. They usually
omit iron (taste and safety), carry token minerals, and contain 2-4 g of added
sugar per serving. Nutrient stability is poorer than in tablets, so manufacturers
add overages; ConsumerLab has repeatedly found gummies with nearly double the
labelled folate [R51].

Bioequivalence is not the problem. Industry-sponsored crossover studies found
equivalent total absorption of vitamin E, folate and B12 from gummies and tablets,
with folate peaking earlier from the gummy [R62]. The problem is what else is in
the gummy and what is missing.

Powders such as AG1 and Huel Daily Greens are multivitamins plus fibre, probiotics
and powdered plants. AG1 supplies no vitamin D, 1 mg iron, and 7.4 g of
unquantified blends for $3.30 per serving. Huel supplies a more complete vitamin
profile, 126 mcg MK-7 and 2.7 g of blends for $2.00. Both cost 8-30 times a tablet
that delivers similar vitamins.

## 5. Ingredient forms and bioavailability

The full analysis is in [`docs/forms-and-bioavailability.md`](../docs/forms-and-bioavailability.md).
The findings that change what you get:

### 5.1 Vitamin D3 versus D2

A randomised trial in 107 adults compared 50 mcg/day of D2, D3 or placebo for eight
weeks in winter. D3 raised 25(OH)D3 from 41.5 to 88.0 nmol/L. D2 raised 25(OH)D2
but lowered 25(OH)D3 from 36.4 to 16.6 nmol/L, and total 25(OH)D was significantly
lower than with D3 [R67]. D3 is the correct form for a supplement.

### 5.2 Folate: folic acid and 5-MTHF

Folic acid is synthetic, stable, about 85% bioavailable, and must be reduced and
methylated. 5-MTHF is already reduced. Short-term bioavailability studies find them
equivalent at equal doses [R64]. Some trials find 5-MTHF raises plasma folate more,
particularly in women with the MTHFR 677TT genotype [R65], and EFSA assigns it a
higher conversion factor at doses above 400 mcg [R66].

No trial shows better clinical outcomes with 5-MTHF. It does avoid unmetabolised
folic acid in plasma, which is a reasonable reason to prefer it, especially at
doses above 400 mcg. It is not a reason to pay three times more for a multivitamin.

### 5.3 Vitamin B12

Cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin and hydroxocobalamin all
converge on the same intracellular pools. Cyanocobalamin is stable, cheap and
effective; methylcobalamin is not superior for the general population [R68]. The
"active B12" marketing is mostly about a rare conversion defect.

### 5.4 Vitamin E

Natural *d*-alpha-tocopherol has about twice the biological activity of synthetic
*dl*-alpha per mg [R29]. Labels in mg alpha-tocopherol equivalent already correct
for this. Paying more for natural E in a multivitamin buys a smaller number on the
label, not more vitamin E.

### 5.5 Minerals

- **Iron:** ferrous sulfate is the reference treatment but causes more GI effects;
  ferrous bisglycinate is similarly effective with fewer symptoms [R26].
- **Calcium:** citrate absorbs without gastric acid; carbonate needs food [R22].
- **Magnesium:** oxide is about 4% absorbed versus 9-11% for soluble salts [R22].
- **Zinc:** citrate and chelates are modestly better absorbed than oxide; at 10-15
  mg the difference is small, and copper balance matters more [R24].
- **Selenium:** selenomethionine is retained better than selenite [R22].

### 5.6 The bottom line on forms

For most nutrients, the form is a second-order issue compared with the dose and the
reason for taking the product. The exceptions that matter are vitamin D3 versus D2,
iron salts for tolerability, magnesium oxide versus soluble salts, and the
zinc-copper balance. The rest is mostly marketing.

## 6. Dosage, upper limits and the arithmetic of overlap

### 6.1 The upper limits that matter

| Nutrient | Adult UL | Critical effect | Relevance to multis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin A (preformed) | 3,000 mcg RAE | Teratogenicity, hepatotoxicity | Some sports multis hit it |
| Vitamin D | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) | Hypercalcaemia | Stacking a multi with a high-dose D capsule |
| Vitamin E | 1,000 mg | Haemorrhage | Rarely reached by a multi |
| Niacin | 35 mg | Flushing, hepatotoxicity | High-potency multis exceed it |
| Vitamin B6 | 100 mg | Sensory neuropathy | High-potency multis approach it |
| Folic acid | 1,000 mcg | Masking B12 deficiency | Prenatal and high-dose products |
| Iron | 45 mg | GI effects, overload | 18 mg is 40% of the UL |
| Zinc | 40 mg | Copper deficiency | 25 mg is 62% of the UL |
| Selenium | 400 mcg | Selenosis | 200 mcg is 50% of the UL |
| Iodine | 1,100 mcg | Thyroid dysfunction | Kelp and prenatal products |
| Calcium | 2,000 mg | Hypercalcaemia, GI | A multi's 200-300 mg is minor |
| Magnesium | 350 mg (supplemental) | Diarrhoea | A multi's 20-100 mg is minor |

Source: IOM and EFSA [R22; R23]. The full table, with notes, is in
[`docs/generated/upper-limits.md`](../docs/generated/upper-limits.md).

### 6.2 What the sample shows

Of 20 products, three exceed an adult UL for at least one nutrient:

| Product | Nutrient | % of UL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thorne Basic Nutrients 2/Day | Niacin 80 mg | 229% |
| Opti-Men | Niacin 75 mg; vitamin A 3,000 mcg | 214%; 100% |
| Life Extension One-Per-Day | Niacin 50 mg | 143% |

Six more reach 50-69% of a UL for at least one nutrient (zinc, niacin, vitamin D).
That is not automatically a reason to avoid them, but it is a reason to check what
else the person is taking. Opti-Men's vitamin A is declared as beta-carotene and
mixed carotenoids, so its 100% figure is an upper bound rather than a preformed
retinol exposure.

### 6.3 Worked example: the "immune stack"

| Product | Vitamin D | Zinc | Vitamin C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Multivitamin | 25 mcg | 11 mg | 90 mg |
| Vitamin D3 capsule | 125 mcg | — | — |
| Zinc lozenge, 4/day | — | 40 mg | — |
| Vitamin C 1,000 mg | — | — | 1,000 mg |
| **Total** | **150 mcg** | **51 mg** | **1,090 mg** |
| **UL** | **100 mcg** | **40 mg** | **2,000 mg** |

Vitamin D is 150% of the UL and zinc 128%. Neither is an emergency, but this is a
regimen that should be deliberate and time-limited. Vitamin D toxicity generally
requires sustained intakes above 10,000 IU (250 mcg), so 6,000 IU is unlikely to
cause harm; the zinc is the more immediate problem because it will deplete copper
over weeks.

### 6.4 Worked example: B vitamins

A high-potency multi (niacin 80 mg, B6 20 mg) plus a B-complex (niacin 50 mg, B6
50 mg) gives 130 mg niacin and 70 mg B6. Niacin is 3.7 times the UL; B6 is 70% of
it. Add a "stress" formula with another 50 mg B6 and the person is at 120 mg, the
range where neuropathy appears. The symptoms — numbness, tingling, unsteadiness —
are easy to attribute to something else and slow to resolve.

### 6.5 The principle

Add up every source before adding another product. The label arithmetic is simple,
and it is the most common avoidable harm in the category.

## 7. What the randomised evidence shows

### 7.1 Mortality

Nine pooled RCTs (n=51,550) found no reduction in all-cause mortality: OR 0.94
(0.87-1.01) [R02]. The three largest trials dominate:

- **PHS II:** 14,641 male physicians, Centrum Silver, median 11.2 years. Total
  mortality HR 0.94 (0.88-1.02) [R03].
- **COSMOS:** 21,442 adults (women 65+, men 60+), Centrum Silver, median 3.6 years.
  All-cause mortality HR 0.93 (0.81-1.08) [R05].
- **SU.VI.MAX:** 13,017 French adults, five-ingredient antioxidant mixture, 7.5
  years. Mortality RR 0.77 (0.57-1.00) overall; men 0.63 (0.42-0.93) [R10].

None was individually significant. The pooled estimate is consistent with anything
from a 13% reduction to a 1% increase.

A 2024 pooled analysis of three US cohorts (n=390,124, 20+ years) found daily users
had a 4% higher mortality risk (HR 1.04, 1.02-1.07), consistent in a time-varying
analysis [R09]. This is observational and confounded by reverse causation, but it
is incompatible with a large benefit.

**Interpretation:** no mortality benefit. The honest confidence interval still
allows a small benefit or a small harm.

### 7.2 Cardiovascular disease

PHS II randomised 14,641 men to Centrum Silver or placebo. Major cardiovascular
events: 745 versus 728, HR 1.02 (0.92-1.13). There was no effect on MI, stroke or
cardiovascular death [R04].

COSMOS found a composite CVD HR of 0.98 (0.86-1.12) [R05]. Pooled CVD mortality
across four trials was OR 0.94 (0.83-1.06) [R02].

This is one of the best-tested questions in nutrition, and the answer is null. A
multivitamin is not a cardiovascular intervention. Blood pressure, smoking, lipids,
glucose, diet pattern and exercise are.

### 7.3 Cancer

The only positive randomised signal, and smaller than it looks.

**Pooled incidence:** OR 0.93 (0.87-0.99), four RCTs, n=48,859. Absolute risk
difference among adequately powered trials: −0.2% to −1.2% [R02].

**PHS II:** 17.0 versus 18.3 cancers per 1,000 person-years, HR 0.92 (0.86-0.998),
P=0.04. Total epithelial cancers HR 0.92. Cancers other than prostate HR 0.88
(0.79-0.98). Prostate cancer, about half of all events, was unaffected. Cancer
mortality HR 0.88 (0.77-1.01) [R03].

**COSMOS:** HR 0.97 (0.86-1.09), null. Lung cancer HR 0.62 (0.42-0.92); colorectal
HR 1.30 (0.80-2.12); breast HR 1.06 (0.79-1.42) [R05].

**SU.VI.MAX:** overall RR 0.90 (0.76-1.06). Men RR 0.69 (0.53-0.91); women RR 1.04
(0.85-1.29); interaction P=0.02 [R10].

**Linxian:** in a population with endemic oesophageal and gastric cancer and
nutritional deficiencies, a combination of beta-carotene, vitamin E and selenium
reduced total mortality (RR 0.91, 0.84-0.99) and stomach cancer mortality (RR 0.79,
0.64-0.99) over 5.25 years [R11]. At 35 years, the overall mortality effect was gone
(HR 0.94, 0.85-1.04) [R12]. This is a deficiency-correction story, not a
multivitamin story.

**Cancer mortality:** OR 0.94 (0.81-1.09), no significant reduction.

**Interpretation:** a small reduction in cancer incidence, no reduction in cancer
death, inconsistent across trials and subgroups, and driven largely by one trial in
male physicians. It is not a reason to take a multivitamin, and it is not a reason
to expect protection.

### 7.4 Cognition

Three COSMOS substudies found a consistent small benefit in older adults:

| Substudy | n | Assessment | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| COSMOS-Mind | 2,262 | Telephone | Global cognition +0.07 SD (0.02-0.12); memory and executive function also better |
| COSMOS-Web | 3,562 | Web battery | Immediate recall +0.27 words versus placebo; no effect on retention or executive function |
| COSMOS-Clinic | 573 | In-person | Global +0.06 SU; episodic memory +0.12 SU; executive function +0.04 SU (NS) |
| Meta-analysis | 5,200 | Mixed | Global +0.07 SU (0.03-0.11); episodic memory +0.06 (0.03-0.10) |

The meta-analytic effect is equivalent to roughly 2 years less cognitive aging
(3.1 years by the COSMOS-Web calibration) [R06; R07; R08].

Against that, PHS II followed 5,947 men for 12 years and found no effect on global
cognition (−0.01 SU, −0.04 to 0.02) or verbal memory (−0.005 SU, −0.04 to 0.03)
[R13]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (n=13,600) found a small effect on delayed
free recall (SMD 0.09) and nothing on other domains [R16].

**Interpretation:** a real but small effect in older adults, not replicated outside
COSMOS, and absent in the longest trial. It is the most interesting recent finding
in the field and the least ready for a public recommendation.

### 7.5 Immunity and infection

The evidence is weaker than the marketing.

A systematic review of eight RCTs in older people concluded the evidence was poor
to moderate quality, heterogeneous and conflicting, and did not support routine use
in all older people [R36]. The largest nursing-home trial (n=748, mean age 85)
found no overall reduction in infections: OR 0.92 (0.82-1.03); a post hoc subgroup
without dementia had fewer infections (RR 0.81, 0.66-0.99) [R34].

A small trial in adults with type 2 diabetes and subclinical deficiency found fewer
infection days and less absenteeism (n=130) [R35]. A rapid review of seven RCTs
found no reduction in acute respiratory infection incidence, possible reduction in
some symptoms, and insufficient evidence for COVID-19 [R16; R30].

**Interpretation:** no reliable prevention in well-nourished adults. Possible
benefit in older or subclinically deficient populations, low certainty.

### 7.6 Energy and fatigue

No randomised evidence supports an energy benefit in replete adults. A prospective
Swiss cohort found no association between multivitamin use and fatigue severity or
incidence [R16]. B-vitamin supplementation does not improve fatigue, cognition or
depression in people without deficiency.

The mechanism is simple: B vitamins are cofactors, not fuel. Adding cofactor to a
system that already has enough does not increase flux.

### 7.7 Pregnancy

Folic acid is the strongest evidence in the field. The MRC Vitamin Study randomised
1,817 women with a previous affected pregnancy to 4 mg folic acid or placebo and
found a 72% reduction in neural tube defects [R37]. Czeizel and Dudás found a
similar effect for first occurrence with a multivitamin containing 0.8 mg folic acid
[R38]. The USPSTF recommends 0.4-0.8 mg/day for all women planning or capable of
pregnancy.

Prenatal multis also supply iodine, iron and vitamin D. Observational and
meta-analytic data suggest maternal multivitamin use may reduce paediatric cancer
risk, but confounding is likely and certainty is very low [R16].

### 7.8 Eyes

The AREDS formula (500 mg vitamin C, 400 IU vitamin E, 15 mg beta-carotene, 80 mg
zinc, 2 mg copper) reduced progression from intermediate to advanced AMD by about
25-28% (OR 0.72, 0.58-0.90; about 78 fewer cases per 1,000 over 6 years) [R47;
R48]. AREDS2 replaced beta-carotene with 10 mg lutein and 2 mg zeaxanthin; the
primary analysis was not significant (HR 0.90, 0.76-1.07), but lutein/zeaxanthin
was better than beta-carotene in secondary analyses (HR 0.82, 0.69-0.96) and is
safer for smokers [R46].

This is not a general multivitamin. The doses are 5-10 times higher, and the benefit
is limited to people with intermediate AMD. There is no benefit for prevention in
people without AMD.

### 7.9 Blood pressure and other outcomes

A rapid review of meta-analyses reported small reductions in systolic blood
pressure with multivitamin use in at-risk populations [R16]. The effect is too small
and too inconsistently measured to be a treatment for hypertension.

No consistent benefit has been shown for visual acuity, COVID-19 outcomes, frailty,
biological aging, hair growth or exercise performance.

## 8. Interpreting the evidence: five traps

### 8.1 Relative versus absolute

"Reduces cancer risk by 7%" is the relative figure. The absolute difference is
about 2 to 12 fewer cancers per 1,000 people over 3 to 11 years. Both are true; one
is used to sell, the other to decide.

### 8.2 Subgroups and multiplicity

PHS II found a larger cancer benefit in men with a baseline cancer history (HR 0.73)
and in non-prostate cancers. SU.VI.MAX found a benefit in men but not women.
COSMOS found a lung-cancer benefit but a colorectal signal in the wrong direction.
These are subgroup findings in trials not powered for them. Some are real; many are
noise. A review that reports only the favourable subgroup is marketing.

### 8.3 The supplement is not the population

The trials tested Centrum Silver, a five-ingredient antioxidant mixture, and a
Chinese deficiency-correction formula. They did not test the premium capsules,
gummies or greens powders that dominate advertising. A null result for Centrum
Silver is not a null result for every product, and a positive result for a
deficiency-correction formula in Linxian is not evidence for a well-fed American.

### 8.4 Healthy-user confounding

People who take supplements are healthier, richer and more health-conscious. Cohort
studies that find benefits are usually measuring that. The 2024 mortality cohort
found the opposite direction, which is a useful reminder that observational
estimates can go either way depending on adjustment.

### 8.5 Surrogate endpoints

"Supports immune health", "supports energy metabolism" and "antioxidant power" are
not outcomes. They are mechanisms or assays. The outcomes that matter are infections,
fatigue, heart attacks, cancer, cognition and death. On those, the evidence is
small or null.

## 9. Safety

### 9.1 The harm signals that are real

**Beta-carotene in smokers.** ATBC: 20 mg/day in 29,133 male smokers increased lung
cancer incidence by 18% (RR 1.18, 1.03-1.36) [R19]. CARET: 30 mg/day beta-carotene
plus 7,500 mcg RAE retinol in 18,314 smokers, former smokers and asbestos-exposed
men increased lung cancer by 28% (RR 1.28, 1.04-1.57), lung cancer death by 46%,
and all-cause mortality by 17% [R20]. Pooled CVD mortality OR 1.10 (1.02-1.19)
[R02]. The doses are 10-30 times a multivitamin's beta-carotene, and the population
is high-risk. A standard multi has not been shown to cause this.

**High-dose vitamin E.** Pooled trials at usual doses show no mortality benefit
(OR 1.02, 0.97-1.07). An earlier meta-analysis found a small excess at 400 IU/day
or more [R41], and SELECT found a 17% increase in prostate cancer with 400 IU/day
[R42]. A multi's 15-30 mg is not the exposure.

**Iron.** GI effects are common; overload is dangerous in haemochromatosis; overdose
is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under six [R26].

**Zinc without copper.** 50 mg/day for weeks causes copper deficiency [R24; R25].

**Vitamin B6.** Above 100 mg/day causes sensory neuropathy; cases are reported below
it [R22].

**Niacin.** Above 35 mg/day is above the UL; gram doses are hepatotoxic [R22].

**Vitamin A.** Preformed retinol is teratogenic and hepatotoxic at 3,000 mcg RAE/day
[R21].

**Selenium.** 200 mcg/day was associated with an 11% relative increase in type 2
diabetes in a meta-analysis of trials [R43]; the UL is 400 mcg.

**Folic acid.** 1,000 mcg/day can mask B12 deficiency. A small trial found a
2.6-fold increase in prostate cancer with 1 mg/day (HR 2.63, 1.03-6.60, n=643)
[R39]; it has not been replicated.

**Calcium.** The cardiovascular signal is contested. A 2010 meta-analysis raised
concern [R44]; a 2023 meta-analysis of 11 trials found no significant excess of MI,
CHD, stroke or mortality [R45]. A multi's 100-300 mg is far below the 1 g/day tested.

### 9.2 What is not a real risk at standard doses

- Multivitamins do not cause cancer, heart disease or death at standard doses. The
  trials are large and long enough to exclude common serious harms.
- Water-soluble vitamins are not automatically safe. B6, niacin and vitamin C have
  real toxicity at high doses.
- "Toxins" and "fillers" are not a useful frame. The documented problems are
  sub-potency, overage, poor disintegration and heavy-metal contamination, not
  mysterious poisons.

### 9.3 Contamination and label accuracy

Independent testing finds failures in roughly one quarter to one third of products:

- **ConsumerLab 2026:** 7 of 22 (32%) failed. One contained 11.8% of its claimed
  vitamin A; some contained up to four times the labelled folate or zinc; one had
  almost twice the labelled vitamin D [R52].
- **ConsumerLab 2025:** 8 of 29 (27.6%) failed. Three gummies had nearly double the
  labelled folic acid; a prenatal had 88% more iodine than labelled; one product had
  14% of its expected vitamin D; one tablet failed disintegration [R51].
- **USDA disintegration survey:** 92.6% of 94 samples passed; all capsules passed;
  7 of 77 tablets failed [R71].
- **Prenatal heavy-metal survey (2025):** across 47 products, lead was detectable in
  68%, cadmium in 62% and arsenic in 15%, but none exceeded USP permitted daily
  exposure limits. Iodine was under-labelled by more than 20% in 80% of the products
  that declared it [R60].

The pattern is consistent: most products are fine, a minority are not, and the
failures are not predictable from price or brand size.

### 9.4 Special populations

- **Pregnancy:** folic acid 400-800 mcg; avoid preformed vitamin A above 3,000 mcg
  RAE and high-dose beta-carotene; iodine 150 mcg; iron as needed.
- **Older adults:** lower acid reduces absorption of calcium carbonate, iron and
  food-bound B12; citrate, bisglycinate and crystalline B12 are better.
- **Chronic kidney disease:** potassium, magnesium, phosphorus and some vitamins
  accumulate; use nephrology guidance.
- **Liver disease:** avoid high-dose niacin and preformed vitamin A.
- **Haemochromatosis:** avoid iron.
- **Bariatric surgery:** lifelong MVM is standard of care, often at higher doses.
- **Children:** adult multis are not children's products; iron overdose is
  particularly dangerous. Keep all supplements out of reach.
- **Pets:** human multis can be toxic to dogs and cats; some gummies contain xylitol.

## 10. Interactions

The clinically important interactions are few and manageable:

| Drug | Interaction | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Warfarin | Vitamin K changes INR; high-dose vitamin E and C may add bleeding risk | Keep vitamin K intake stable; coordinate before switching products |
| Levothyroxine | Calcium, iron and magnesium reduce absorption | Separate by 4 hours |
| Tetracyclines, quinolones | Minerals chelate the antibiotic | Separate by 2-6 hours |
| Proton pump inhibitors | Reduce food-bound B12 and iron absorption | Crystalline B12 is absorbed; monitor |
| Metformin | Lowers B12 status | Measure B12 periodically |
| Methotrexate | Folate antagonism is therapeutic | Do not add high-dose folate without oncology guidance |
| Isotretinoin/acitretin | Additive retinoid toxicity | Avoid preformed vitamin A supplements |
| Laboratory immunoassays | High-dose biotin causes false results | Stop 48-72 hours before tests |

Nutrient-nutrient interactions matter too: calcium and iron compete, iron at 25 mg
or more reduces zinc absorption, and zinc at 50 mg or more reduces copper. A
well-designed multi avoids therapeutic doses of competing minerals in the same
pill, which is an argument for a moderate-dose multi plus targeted singles rather
than an "everything at maximum" formula.

## 11. Product quality and certification

### 11.1 What certification means

| Certification | What it verifies | What it does not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| USP Verified | Identity, potency, purity, dissolution, GMP | Efficacy, formula quality |
| NSF/ANSI 173 | Label content, contaminants | Efficacy |
| NSF Certified for Sport | Above plus banned substances | Efficacy |
| Informed Choice/Sport | Banned substances, batch testing | Full label accuracy |
| USDA Organic | Production method | Purity, potency |
| Non-GMO Project | Genetic material | Anything about vitamins |

A seal means the bottle contains what it says it contains. It does not mean the
formula is well designed or that the product improves health. A certified product
can still have 80 mg of niacin or no copper alongside 25 mg of zinc.

### 11.2 The sample

Six of 20 products carry a third-party certification. The certified group's median
cost is higher, but the cheapest certified product ($0.11) is cheaper than most
uncertified ones. Certification is not expensive; premium branding is.

## 13. Who benefits, who does not, who is harmed

### 13.1 Clear benefit

- **Pregnancy and preconception.** Folic acid reduces neural tube defects by about
  72%. This is the strongest evidence in the field.
- **Documented deficiency.** A multivitamin treats it, though often the wrong tool
  for the therapeutic phase.
- **Bariatric surgery.** Lifelong MVM is standard of care.
- **Malabsorption.** Coeliac disease, Crohn's, cystic fibrosis, pancreatitis and
  short bowel syndrome.

### 13.2 Probable benefit

- **Vegans and strict vegetarians.** B12 is the critical nutrient; deficiency
  prevalence ranges from 5% to 52% depending on the population and cut-off.
- **Older adults with poor or restricted intake.** Modest benefit; the infection
  evidence is weak, the cognitive signal small.
- **Food insecurity.** Benefit scales with the dietary gap.
- **Alcohol use disorder.** Thiamine, folate, magnesium and zinc are commonly low.
- **Intermediate AMD.** The AREDS2 formula, not a standard multi.
- **People on nutrient-depleting drugs.** Metformin, PPIs, diuretics, isoniazid.

### 13.3 Unlikely benefit

- **Healthy adults eating a varied diet.** The largest user group and the smallest
  expected benefit.
- **Athletes.** No ergogenic effect in well-nourished athletes.
- **Fatigue.** No effect; investigate the cause.
- **Cold prevention.** No reliable effect in well-nourished adults.
- **Smokers taking beta-carotene.** Possible harm, not benefit.

### 13.4 Possible harm

- Smokers and asbestos-exposed people taking high-dose beta-carotene.
- Men and postmenopausal women taking iron without a reason.
- Anyone stacking high-dose zinc without copper.
- Anyone stacking B6 or niacin above the UL.
- People with kidney disease.
- Children in households with adult supplements.
- People who delay diagnosis because they are "taking a multivitamin".

## 14. Targeted supplementation versus a general multivitamin

For a person with a specific, measurable need, targeted supplementation is better.
It delivers a therapeutic dose, avoids unnecessary ingredients, and can be
monitored. A multivitamin cannot treat vitamin D deficiency (25 mcg versus 125 mcg),
iron deficiency anaemia (18 mg versus 60-120 mg) or B12 deficiency with neurological
signs (6-25 mcg versus 1,000 mcg).

For a person with an unmeasured, diffuse risk of poor intake, a general multi is a
reasonable hedge. It is cheap, convenient and covers many nutrients at once.

The practical rule: **target the nutrients you have a reason to target, and use a
multi only for the residual, unmeasured gap.** For pregnancy, bariatric surgery,
malabsorption or a documented deficiency, the targeted product is not optional and
the multi is the supporting cast. For a healthy adult with a good diet, the residual
gap is small enough that the multi is optional.

The failure modes are symmetrical: using a general multi to treat a specific
deficiency, and using five targeted products to do a job one cheap multi would do
while stacking overlapping nutrients.

A fuller decision framework is in
[`docs/targeted-vs-general.md`](../docs/targeted-vs-general.md).

## 15. Practical recommendations

These assume the person and clinician have decided that some supplementation is
appropriate. They are not a recommendation to start.

1. **Define the goal.** If it is a symptom (fatigue, hair loss, brain fog), a
   multivitamin is the wrong tool. If it is a known deficiency, treat the
   deficiency. If it is "insurance", accept that the expected benefit is small.
2. **Check for a reason to need more.** Pregnancy, vegan diet, bariatric surgery,
   malabsorption, alcohol use disorder, poor intake, nutrient-depleting drugs.
3. **Choose the formula for the person.** Iron-free for men and postmenopausal
   women; 8-18 mg iron for menstruating women with low ferritin; prenatal with
   folate, iodine, iron, D3 and choline for pregnancy; AREDS2 for intermediate AMD.
4. **Check five numbers.** Vitamin D (at least 25 mcg, as D3); zinc (8-15 mg, with
   copper if above 15 mg); B6 (under 50 mg); niacin (under 35 mg); iron (only if
   needed).
5. **Buy certification, not story.** USP, NSF/ANSI 173, NSF Certified for Sport or
   Informed Choice/Sport. Skip unquantified blends.
6. **Do the arithmetic.** Add the multi to every other supplement and compare with
   the ULs. The common failures are vitamin D, zinc, B6, niacin and iron.
7. **Do not expect outcomes the trials do not show.** No mortality benefit, no CVD
   benefit, no energy benefit, no cold prevention. A small cancer-incidence signal
   and a small cognitive signal are not a reason on their own.
8. **Reassess.** A multivitamin is not a lifetime commitment by default. If the diet
   improves, the indication may disappear. If symptoms persist, investigate.

## 16. Limitations and research agenda

### 16.1 Limitations of this review

- Twenty products is a purposive sample, not a census.
- Prices are US snapshots and will change.
- The trials tested a narrow slice of the market; premium formats are largely
  untested for outcomes.
- Follow-up in the largest modern trial (COSMOS) was 3.6 years, short for cancer
  and cardiovascular disease.
- Populations were narrow: male physicians and mostly white, educated older adults.
- Industry funding and publication bias affect the underlying literature.
- No new laboratory assays were commissioned.
- The review is not peer reviewed.

### 16.2 What would change the conclusion

- A large trial of a modern premium formula with a hard endpoint.
- Replication of the COSMOS cognitive finding in an independent cohort.
- A trial of multivitamins in a genuinely deficient Western population.
- Longer follow-up of COSMOS for cancer mortality.
- Better data on whether genotype-guided folate or B12 choice changes outcomes.
- A regulatory requirement for batch-level certification, which would reduce the
  quality problem without changing the efficacy question.

### 16.3 Research agenda

1. **Replicate COSMOS cognition** in a diverse population with a pre-registered
   primary endpoint and a longer duration.
2. **Test whether the cancer-incidence signal is real** by pooling PHS II, COSMOS
   and SU.VI.MAX individual-patient data with a pre-specified analysis.
3. **Measure what premium products actually deliver**, including disintegration,
   overage and contamination, across lots rather than one sample.
4. **Study the deficiency-correction hypothesis directly:** does a multivitamin
   improve outcomes in people with measured low status, and not in people with
   normal status?
5. **Quantify the harm of stacking** with real-world supplement-use data and
   adverse-event reports.

## 17. Conclusions

1. **Multivitamins are a small-effect intervention with a large marketing budget.**
   The trials are consistent: no mortality benefit, no cardiovascular benefit, a
   small and fragile cancer-incidence signal, a small cognitive signal in one recent
   trial, and no energy or infection benefit in well-nourished adults.

2. **The benefit is concentrated in people with increased needs or poor intake.**
   Pregnancy, documented deficiency, bariatric surgery, malabsorption, vegan diets
   and food insecurity are the situations where the case is strong. For a healthy,
   well-fed adult, the expected benefit is close to zero.

3. **Form and brand matter less than dose and indication.** Vitamin D3 over D2, a
   sensible zinc-copper balance, B6 and niacin below the UL, and iron only when
   needed are the decisions that matter. Whole-food matrices, K2, CoQ10 and
   proprietary blends are price, not evidence.

4. **The category has a real quality problem.** Roughly one quarter to one third of
   tested products fail label or dissolution standards. Certification reduces but
   does not eliminate the risk. A cheap certified tablet is a better buy than an
   expensive uncertified powder.

5. **Targeted supplementation is usually better than a general multi when a need is
   known.** The general multi is a hedge against an unknown gap, not a treatment.

6. **The honest summary is unglamorous.** A multivitamin is not a shortcut to
   health, and it is not a scam. It is a cheap, convenient, low-risk way to cover
   some nutrient gaps, with a small chance of benefit and a smaller chance of harm,
   and a large amount of money spent on marketing that oversells both.
