# Targeted supplementation versus a general multivitamin

The choice is usually framed as "multivitamin or nothing". The better framing is
"general coverage or specific correction". They solve different problems.

- A **general multivitamin** is insurance against a broad, unknown gap. It spreads
  a small dose across many nutrients. It is cheap, convenient and imprecise.
- **Targeted supplementation** addresses a known gap: a low 25(OH)D, a vegan diet,
  pregnancy, iron deficiency, malabsorption. It uses a higher dose of the nutrient
  that is actually needed and avoids the ones that are not.

For most people with a specific, measurable need, targeted is better. For people
with an unmeasured, diffuse risk of poor intake, a general multi is a reasonable
hedge. The mistake is using a general multi to treat a specific deficiency, or
using five targeted products to do a job one cheap multi would do.

---

## The case for targeted

### 1. Dose

A multivitamin cannot supply therapeutic doses of most nutrients. It has 25 mcg of
vitamin D; a deficient person may need 125 mcg (5,000 IU) for 8-12 weeks. It has
18 mg iron; iron deficiency anaemia needs 60-120 mg of elemental iron. It has
20-100 mg magnesium; a therapeutic magnesium dose is 200-400 mg. It has 6-25 mcg
B12; B12 deficiency with neurological signs needs 1,000 mcg.

If the goal is correction, the multi is the wrong dose.

### 2. Signal-to-noise

Every additional ingredient is an additional opportunity for a wrong dose, an
interaction, or an unnecessary exposure. A man with normal iron status does not
need 18 mg iron. A person with adequate zinc does not need 24 mg zinc and no
copper. A person with normal B6 status does not need 75 mg. Targeted products let
you avoid the ingredients you do not need.

### 3. Cost per effective unit

If you need vitamin D, the relevant comparison is cost per 25 mcg of D3, not cost
per tablet. In this sample, a dedicated D3 product costs a few cents per 1,000 IU;
paying for a premium multi to get the same D3 is inefficient unless you also want
the other nutrients.

### 4. Measurability

Targeted supplementation can be monitored. 25(OH)D, ferritin, B12, folate and TSH
are measurable. "General wellness" is not. A treated deficiency has a before and
after; a multi usually does not.

---

## The case for a general multi

### 1. You do not know the gap

Most people have not measured their micronutrient status. Diet recalls are
unreliable, and the deficiencies that matter are often subclinical. Population
data show widespread shortfalls: 94% of Americans below the EAR for vitamin D from
food, 88.5% for vitamin E, 52% for magnesium, 44% for calcium, 43% for vitamin A
and 39% for vitamin C. A multi covers many of these at once, imperfectly.

### 2. Adherence and simplicity

One tablet is easier than five. For people who will not take a targeted regimen
consistently, a multi that is actually taken beats a perfect stack that is not.

### 3. Pregnancy and bariatric surgery

These are situations where several nutrients are needed at once and a
condition-specific multi is the standard of care. Targeted single nutrients would
mean five bottles.

### 4. Cost

A certified general multi costs $0.11-$0.25 per day. Five targeted products cost
$1-$3 per day. For food-insecure households, the multi is the realistic option.

## A practical decision framework

### Step 1: Is there a known or highly likely deficiency?

| Situation | Test or trigger | Targeted action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low sun exposure, dark skin, winter, older age | 25(OH)D | Vitamin D3 25-50 mcg/day maintenance; higher for correction |
| Vegan or strict vegetarian | Diet history, B12 | B12 25-100 mcg/day, or a multi with B12 |
| Heavy menstrual bleeding | Ferritin, CBC | Iron with vitamin C; investigate the cause |
| Pregnancy | Pregnancy plan | Prenatal MVM with folate, iodine, iron, D3 |
| Bariatric surgery | Surgical history | Bariatric-specific MVM with monitoring |
| Coeliac, Crohn's, pancreatitis | Diagnosis | Disease treatment plus monitored replacement |
| Metformin, PPI, warfarin | Medication list | Targeted B12, iron, or stable vitamin K |
| Alcohol use disorder | History | Thiamine, folate, magnesium; clinical care |

If yes: treat the specific need first. A general multi can be added for coverage,
but it is not the treatment.

### Step 2: If there is no known deficiency, what is the actual goal?

- **"I want insurance against a bad diet."** A cheap certified multi is a
  reasonable choice. Expect a small benefit at best.
- **"I want more energy."** A multi will not provide it. Investigate sleep, iron,
  thyroid, mood and medication.
- **"I want to prevent heart disease or cancer."** A multi does not do this. Blood
  pressure, smoking, lipids, glucose, diet pattern and exercise do.
- **"I want to live longer."** No randomised evidence supports a mortality benefit,
  and one large cohort found a small increase in mortality among users.
- **"I want to protect my memory."** The COSMOS signal is small (about 0.07 SD,
  roughly 2 years less cognitive aging) and unreplicated outside that trial. It is
  not a reason on its own, but it is the most interesting recent finding.

### Step 3: If you take a multi, choose it for your situation

| Situation | What to look for | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Man, or postmenopausal woman | Iron-free, D3 ≥ 25 mcg, zinc 8-15 mg with copper, B6 < 50 mg | 18 mg iron, 25 mg+ zinc without copper |
| Menstruating woman | Iron 8-18 mg if ferritin is low, D3 ≥ 25 mcg, folate 400 mcg | Iron if ferritin is normal |
| Pregnancy | 400-800 mcg folic acid (or 5-MTHF), iodine 150 mcg, iron 27 mg, D3, choline | Preformed vitamin A > 3,000 mcg, high-dose beta-carotene |
| Vegan | B12 ≥ 25 mcg, iodine, zinc, D3 from lichen | Iron unless deficient |
| 50+ | D3 ≥ 25 mcg, B12 ≥ 25 mcg, calcium if intake low, no iron | High-dose beta-carotene if a smoker |
| Smoker | Mixed carotenoids or no beta-carotene, D3 | High-dose beta-carotene |
| On warfarin | Stable vitamin K content, no K2 MK-7 surprises | Large or changing K doses |

### Step 4: Add, don't stack

If you add a targeted nutrient to a multi, do the arithmetic against the UL:

```
total = multi + targeted product + food + any other supplement
```

The common failure modes are vitamin D (multi + 5,000 IU capsule), zinc (multi +
immune lozenges), B6 (multi + B-complex), niacin (multi + B-complex) and iron
(multi + iron tablet).

## Is targeted supplementation actually cheaper?

Sometimes, and sometimes not.

**Cheaper targeted examples:**

- Vitamin D only: a 1,000 IU D3 tablet costs a few cents a day, versus $0.11-$1.33
  for a multi that also delivers nutrients you may not need.
- B12 only (vegan): 1,000 mcg cyanocobalamin tablets cost about $0.02-$0.05/day.
- Folate only (preconception): 400 mcg folic acid costs about $0.02/day. A prenatal
  multi costs $0.20-$0.60/day but also supplies iodine, iron and D3.

**More expensive targeted examples:**

- Five separate products (D, B12, iron, magnesium, zinc) typically cost $0.80-$2.00
  per day and mean five bottles, five adherence decisions and five chances to
  double up.
- A prenatal regimen built from singles is more expensive and less reliable than a
  formulated prenatal.

The practical rule: **target the nutrients you have a reason to target, and use a
multi only for the residual, unmeasured gap.** For a healthy adult with no known
deficiency and a decent diet, the residual gap is small enough that the multi is
optional. For pregnancy, bariatric surgery, malabsorption or a documented
deficiency, the targeted product is not optional and the multi is the supporting
cast.

## What "targeted" should not mean

- **Targeting a symptom with a nutrient.** Fatigue is not a B12 deficiency until
  proven. Hair loss is not a biotin deficiency until proven. "Adrenal support" is
  not a nutrient category.
- **Targeting a genotype with a supplement.** MTHFR testing is used to sell
  methylfolate. 5-MTHF is a reasonable form, but genotype-guided multivitamin
  choice has not been shown to improve outcomes.
- **Targeting a lab value without a clinician.** A low-normal ferritin is not
  automatically an iron indication; iron overload is a real risk.
- **Targeting "optimal" instead of "adequate".** There is no evidence that
  supraphysiological levels of most vitamins are better. For several — vitamin A,
  vitamin E, selenium, beta-carotene — higher is worse.

---

## The short answer

- **Known deficiency or increased need:** targeted, at a therapeutic dose, with
  monitoring. Add a multi only for residual coverage.
- **No known need, good diet:** neither is strongly indicated. If you want
  insurance, a cheap certified multi is a low-risk, low-yield choice.
- **Pregnancy, bariatric surgery, malabsorption:** a condition-specific multi is
  the targeted product.
- **Symptoms:** investigate, do not supplement blindly.
- **Never:** use a general multi to treat a specific deficiency, or stack products
  without adding up the overlapping nutrients.
