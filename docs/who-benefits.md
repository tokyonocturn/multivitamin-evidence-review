# Who benefits, who does not, and who is harmed

The honest answer to "should I take a multivitamin?" is "that depends on who you
are, what you eat, and what you are trying to prevent." This document sorts
populations by strength of evidence, not by enthusiasm.

A useful frame: a multivitamin is a **delivery vehicle for nutrients you are not
getting**. Its value is proportional to the size of the gap it fills. When the gap
is zero, the expected benefit approaches zero and the cost and small risks remain.

---

## 1. Clear benefit (strong evidence, guideline-supported)

### Pregnancy and preconception

**What it does:** Folic acid 400-800 mcg/day started before conception and
continued through the first trimester reduces neural tube defects by about 72%
(MRC Vitamin Study; Czeizel and Dudás). This is the strongest evidence in the
entire multivitamin literature, and it is why prenatal vitamins are recommended
rather than optional.

**What else is in a prenatal:** iodine (150 mcg) for fetal neurodevelopment, iron
(27 mg) for expanded maternal blood volume, D3, B12 and choline. Choline is often
missing or under-dosed, and iodine content is frequently mislabelled.

**Caveats:** Avoid preformed vitamin A above 3,000 mcg RAE. Avoid high-dose
beta-carotene. Folic acid does not replace B12 assessment in women at risk of B12
deficiency.

### Documented deficiency

**What it does:** Treats the deficiency. This sounds trivial, but it is the one
indication where the mechanism, the biomarker and the clinical outcome all line up.

**Examples:** iron deficiency anaemia, B12 deficiency in vegans or pernicious
anaemia, vitamin D deficiency with low 25(OH)D, folate deficiency, zinc deficiency
in malabsorption.

**Caveats:** A multivitamin is often the wrong tool for treatment. Iron deficiency
needs a therapeutic iron dose and a cause investigation; B12 deficiency with
neurological signs needs high-dose B12, not the 6 mcg in a multi. Use a multi for
maintenance after correction, not as the correction.

### Bariatric surgery

**What it does:** Lifelong MVM is standard of care after sleeve gastrectomy and
Roux-en-Y bypass, because the anatomy no longer absorbs enough iron, B12, calcium,
vitamin D, folate and thiamine from food. Deficiencies are common and can be
severe (Wernicke encephalopathy from thiamine deficiency, megaloblastic anaemia
from B12).

**Caveats:** Bariatric products are formulated differently — higher doses, more
bioavailable forms, chewable or liquid. A standard adult multi is usually
insufficient.

### Malabsorption

**What it does:** Provides nutrients that coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, cystic
fibrosis, chronic pancreatitis, short bowel syndrome or extensive gut surgery
prevent from being absorbed.

**Caveats:** The underlying disease needs treatment. Monitoring is required because
some nutrients (fat-soluble vitamins, B12, iron, zinc) may still not be absorbed
even at high doses.

## 2. Probable benefit (moderate evidence, specific circumstances)

### Vegans and strict vegetarians

**What it does:** B12 is the critical one. Vitamin B12 occurs naturally almost
exclusively in animal foods; deficiency prevalence among vegans ranges from 5% to
52% depending on the population and the cut-off used, and regular supplementation
substantially reduces it. A multi supplying 4-25 mcg of B12 covers the need.
Iron, zinc, iodine and long-chain omega-3s are also worth attention.

**Caveats:** B12 is the non-negotiable part. The rest can come from fortified foods
and a well-planned diet. A cheap B12 tablet plus a general multi is often cheaper
than a "vegan multi" with the same ingredients.

### Older adults with poor or restricted intake

**What it does:** Raises micronutrient status in people eating little, or eating a
narrow diet. Low gastric acid reduces absorption of calcium carbonate, iron and
food-bound B12; crystalline B12 and calcium citrate are better choices.

**Caveats:** The evidence for infection reduction is weak and conflicting. The
largest nursing-home trial was null overall (OR 0.92, 0.82-1.03). The cognitive
signal from COSMOS is small. A multi is a reasonable insurance policy for a person
with a genuinely poor diet, not a treatment for aging.

### Food insecurity and very low income

**What it does:** Improves intake adequacy when diet quality is constrained by
cost. The benefit scales with the size of the dietary gap.

**Caveats:** A multivitamin does not provide calories, protein, fibre or essential
fatty acids. It is not a substitute for food assistance. The cheapest certified
products are the right choice here; premium capsules are not.

### People with alcohol use disorder

**What it does:** Alcohol impairs thiamine, folate, B6, magnesium and zinc status.
Thiamine deficiency can cause Wernicke encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome. A
multi is a reasonable adjunct.

**Caveats:** This population often needs higher-dose thiamine and magnesium than a
standard multi provides, and clinical management of withdrawal and nutrition.

### Intermediate age-related macular degeneration

**What it does:** The AREDS/AREDS2 formula — 500 mg vitamin C, 400 IU vitamin E,
80 mg zinc, 2 mg copper, and lutein/zeaxanthin instead of beta-carotene — reduces
progression to advanced AMD by about 28% (OR 0.72, 0.58-0.90), or about 78 fewer
cases per 1,000 people with intermediate AMD over 6 years.

**Caveats:** This is not a standard multivitamin. The doses are much higher, and
the benefit is for people who already have intermediate AMD. It does not prevent
AMD, and it does not help people with early AMD or no AMD. Smokers should use the
AREDS2 formula, not the original beta-carotene version.

### People taking drugs that deplete nutrients

**What it does:** Replaces what the drug removes. Metformin lowers B12; proton pump
inhibitors reduce B12 and iron absorption; diuretics increase magnesium and
potassium loss; isoniazid depletes B6; methotrexate is a folate antagonist (where
folate is given deliberately under supervision).

**Caveats:** This is targeted replacement, not general supplementation. The dose
and monitoring should match the drug. A standard multi may be sufficient for
prevention but not for treatment.

## 3. Unlikely benefit (weak or null evidence in this group)

### Healthy adults eating a varied diet

This is the largest group of users and the group with the least to gain. Across
nine pooled RCTs, all-cause mortality was not reduced (OR 0.94, 0.87-1.01). CVD
mortality was not reduced (OR 0.94, 0.83-1.06). CVD events were not reduced
(PHS II HR 1.02; COSMOS HR 0.98). Cancer mortality was not reduced (OR 0.94,
0.81-1.09).

The one positive signal — cancer incidence, OR 0.93 (0.87-0.99) — is small,
borderline, driven largely by PHS II, and not accompanied by fewer cancer deaths.
The absolute difference is roughly 2 to 12 fewer cancers per 1,000 people over 3 to
11 years.

A 390,124-person cohort found daily multivitamin users had a 4% *higher* mortality
risk (HR 1.04, 1.02-1.07). That is almost certainly confounding — sick people start
supplements — but it is a useful corrective to the assumption that more nutrients
must mean better health.

**Bottom line:** For this group, the expected benefit is small and possibly zero.
The expected cost is $20-$1,200 per year. The expected risk is low but not zero
(iron, zinc, B6, beta-carotene in smokers).

### Athletes and exercisers

No trial shows that a multivitamin improves strength, endurance, VO2 max, recovery
or body composition in well-nourished athletes. Athletes with high energy intakes
usually exceed requirements from food. Those in weight-class sports, with
restrictive eating or with heavy sweating, may need targeted electrolytes and iron,
not a general multi.

### People taking a multivitamin "for energy"

B vitamins are cofactors, not fuel. In replete adults, supplementation does not
reduce fatigue. A prospective cohort found no association between multivitamin use
and fatigue severity or incidence. If fatigue is the problem, the differential is
sleep, anaemia, thyroid disease, depression, sleep apnoea and medication — not a
missing multivitamin.

### People trying to prevent colds

Pooled evidence does not show that multivitamins reduce acute respiratory infection
incidence in well-nourished adults. The nursing-home trial was null. A rapid review
found possible symptom benefit but no incidence benefit, and called the evidence
weak. Zinc lozenges started within 24 hours may shorten a cold by about two days,
but that is a lozenge at the right time, not a daily multi.

### Smokers taking beta-carotene for "antioxidant" protection

This group may be harmed, not helped. See below.

## 4. Possible harm

### Smokers and asbestos-exposed people taking high-dose beta-carotene

Lung cancer risk increased 18% (ATBC, 20 mg/day) and 28% (CARET, 30 mg/day plus
7,500 mcg RAE retinol), with increased all-cause mortality in CARET. This is the
strongest evidence of harm from a supplement in common use.

### Men and postmenopausal women taking iron without a reason

Iron is not needed by most men and postmenopausal women, causes GI effects, and is
dangerous in undiagnosed haemochromatosis. A general multi with 18 mg iron is a
poor default for this group.

### Anyone stacking high-dose zinc without copper

50 mg/day of zinc for weeks can cause copper deficiency. Several immune formulas
sell exactly this.

### Anyone stacking B6 or niacin

B6 above 100 mg/day causes sensory neuropathy; niacin above 35 mg/day is above the
UL and gram doses are hepatotoxic. High-potency multis plus a B-complex can reach
these doses without the person realising.

### People with kidney disease

Magnesium, potassium, phosphorus and some vitamins accumulate. Standard multis are
not designed for reduced kidney function.

### Children in households with adult supplements

Iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under six. Gummies
look like candy. Prenatal vitamins are among the most dangerous products in the
house.

### People who delay diagnosis

A multivitamin can mask B12 deficiency (folate) and can create a false sense of
adequacy. Unexplained fatigue, weight loss, neuropathy or anaemia should be
investigated, not supplemented.

## A decision table

| Person | Does a general MVM help? | Better alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pregnant or planning pregnancy | Yes, for folate and iodine | Prenatal MVM with 400-800 mcg folic acid, iodine, iron, D3; check choline |
| Vegan | Yes, for B12 | B12 25-100 mcg (or a multi), plus iodine and consider algae omega-3 |
| Documented deficiency | Yes, but not as treatment | Therapeutic dose of the deficient nutrient, then maintenance |
| Bariatric surgery | Yes, required | Bariatric-specific MVM with monitoring |
| Malabsorption | Yes | Disease treatment plus monitored, higher-dose replacement |
| Older adult, poor intake | Probably | Cheap certified MVM; calcium/vitamin D if intake low |
| Food insecure | Probably | Cheapest certified MVM; food assistance |
| Healthy adult, varied diet | Marginally, if at all | Nothing, or vitamin D if low sun exposure |
| Athlete, well nourished | No | Food, protein, electrolytes if needed |
| Fatigue | No | Investigate sleep, anaemia, thyroid, mood, medication |
| Intermediate AMD | Not a standard MVM | AREDS2 formula, prescribed by an eye care professional |
| Smoker | No; avoid beta-carotene | Stop smoking; diet; no high-dose beta-carotene |
| Men / postmenopausal women | No, unless deficient | Iron-free MVM if any |
| Kidney disease | No, without supervision | Nephrology-guided replacement |
| On warfarin | Only with INR awareness | Stable vitamin K intake; coordinate with clinic |
