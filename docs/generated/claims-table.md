| ID | Claim | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| natural-e | Natural vitamin E is twice as potent as synthetic. | Supported | Natural d-alpha tocopherol has about twice the biological activity of synthetic dl-alpha per mg. Labels in mg alpha-tocopherol already account for this, so the practical difference is smaller than the marketing implies. |
| prenatal-folate | Prenatal vitamins prevent birth defects. | Supported | Periconceptional folic acid reduces neural tube defects by about 72% in the MRC trial and is recommended at 400-800 mcg/day. This is the strongest evidence for any multivitamin use. |
| beta-carotene-cancer | Beta-carotene causes cancer. | Partially supported | In smokers and asbestos-exposed people, 20-30 mg/day increased lung cancer risk by 18-28%. In non-smokers at multivitamin doses (typically 0.5-3 mg), no such risk has been shown. The claim is true only for the high-dose, high-risk setting. |
| certification-efficacy | A USP or NSF seal proves the multivitamin works. | Partially supported | The seals verify identity, potency, purity and manufacturing quality for the tested lot. They say nothing about whether the product improves health. A certified product can still be a poorly designed formula. |
| gummy-equal | Gummies are just as good as tablets. | Partially supported | Bioequivalence studies show similar total absorption of vitamin E, folate and B12 from gummies and tablets. But gummies are more likely to contain large overages, rarely include iron or meaningful minerals, and usually carry 2-4 g of added sugar per serving. |
| iron-men-harm | Iron in multivitamins is harmful to men. | Partially supported | Most men and postmenopausal women do not need supplemental iron. It causes GI upset and is dangerous in undiagnosed haemochromatosis. There is no consistent evidence that standard 18 mg iron causes cancer or heart disease in healthy men, but the risk-benefit is unfavourable. |
| methylfolate-better | Methylfolate is better than folic acid for everyone. | Partially supported | 5-MTHF and folic acid produce equivalent short-term folate bioavailability, and 5-MTHF raises plasma folate somewhat more in MTHFR TT women. No trial shows better clinical outcomes. 5-MTHF avoids unmetabolised folic acid, which is a reasonable preference but not a proven advantage. |
| mvm-cancer | Multivitamins prevent cancer. | Partially supported | Pooled trials show a small reduction in cancer incidence (OR 0.93, 0.87-0.99), driven by PHS II. There is no reduction in cancer mortality, and the largest modern trial (COSMOS) was null. The absolute benefit is roughly 2 to 12 fewer cancers per 1,000 people over 3 to 11 years. |
| mvm-hair-skin | Multivitamins make hair and nails grow. | Partially supported | Biotin and zinc correct deficiencies that cause hair loss or nail changes. In replete people, supplements have not been shown to increase hair growth, and high-dose biotin can distort thyroid and cardiac lab tests. |
| mvm-immunity | Multivitamins boost immunity and prevent colds. | Partially supported | Trials in older or subclinically deficient adults show fewer infection days in some settings, but the largest nursing-home trial was null and a systematic review called the evidence weak and conflicting. Multis do not prevent acute respiratory infection in well-nourished adults. |
| mvm-kidney-stones | Multivitamins cause kidney stones. | Partially supported | High-dose vitamin C (above 2 g/day) and calcium supplements can raise stone risk in susceptible people. Standard multivitamin doses have not been shown to cause stones. |
| mvm-memory | Multivitamins improve memory in older adults. | Partially supported | Three COSMOS substudies found a consistent but small benefit: about 0.07 SD in global cognition and 0.06 SD in episodic memory, equivalent to roughly 2 years less cognitive aging. PHS II found no effect after 12 years. The effect is real but small and not yet replicated outside COSMOS. |
| mvm-unnecessary | Multivitamins are pointless because diet supplies everything. | Partially supported | For a well-nourished adult eating a varied diet, the expected benefit is small and may be zero. But population intake data show widespread shortfalls for vitamin D, vitamin E, calcium and magnesium, and some groups (vegans, pregnancy, bariatric surgery, malabsorption) cannot meet needs from diet alone. |
| mvm-warfarin-safe | Multivitamins are safe with warfarin. | Partially supported | Small stable amounts of vitamin K (10-25 mcg) are usually tolerated, but formula changes alter INR. K2 MK-7 has a long half-life and is harder to manage. Patients should tell their anticoagulation clinic before starting or switching. |
| mvm-waste | Multivitamins are a waste of money. | Partially supported | For a healthy, well-fed adult, the expected health gain is very small and the annual cost ($20-$1,200) buys little. For pregnancy, deficiency, malabsorption or poor diet, the same product can be high-value. The claim is true as a blanket statement only if you assume everyone is well nourished. |
| zinc-colds | Zinc shortens colds. | Partially supported | Zinc lozenges started within 24 hours may shorten cold duration by about 2 days, with a high rate of nausea and bad taste. A multivitamin's 8-15 mg zinc is not a treatment dose and is not taken at the right time or route. |
| expensive-better | Expensive multivitamins are better. | Unsupported | Cost does not track quality or efficacy. A $0.11 USP-verified tablet and a $3.30 greens powder both supply B vitamins and minerals; the expensive product's extra ingredients are largely unquantified blends. |
| k2-calcium | Vitamin K2 directs calcium into bones and out of arteries. | Unsupported | K2 is required for osteocalcin carboxylation, but trials have not shown that adding K2 to a multivitamin prevents fractures or vascular calcification. K1 status is what matters for clotting, and K interacts with warfarin. |
| methylb12-better | Cyanocobalamin is inferior to methylcobalamin. | Unsupported | All supplemental B12 forms are converted to the same active cobalamins intracellularly. Cyanocobalamin is stable, cheap and effective; methylcobalamin matters mainly in the rare patient who cannot convert cyanocobalamin. |
| mvm-aging | Multivitamins slow aging. | Unsupported | No human trial has shown an effect on biological age, frailty or lifespan. The small cognitive signal in COSMOS is not evidence of slowed aging. |
| mvm-colds | Multivitamins prevent colds. | Unsupported | Pooled trial evidence does not show reduced incidence of acute respiratory infection in well-nourished adults. Zinc lozenges and vitamin C have modest, specific effects that do not transfer to a once-daily multivitamin. |
| mvm-covid | Multivitamins reduce COVID-19 risk or severity. | Unsupported | A rapid review of meta-analyses found no benefit for COVID-19 outcomes. Multis are not a treatment or preventative for SARS-CoV-2. |
| mvm-energy | A daily multivitamin gives you energy. | Unsupported | B vitamins are cofactors in energy metabolism, not fuel. They correct a deficiency if one exists; in replete adults, randomised and prospective data show no effect on fatigue or energy. Caffeine-free multis do not provide calories or stimulants. |
| mvm-everyone | Everyone should take a multivitamin. | Unsupported | No major guideline recommends universal multivitamin use for healthy adults. USPSTF gives an I statement for CVD/cancer prevention. Benefit is concentrated in people with increased needs or poor intake. |
| mvm-heart | Multivitamins prevent heart attacks and strokes. | Unsupported | PHS II found no effect on major cardiovascular events (HR 1.02), and COSMOS found none (HR 0.98). Pooled CVD mortality is null (OR 0.94, 0.83-1.06). |
| mvm-performance | Multivitamins improve exercise performance. | Unsupported | No trial shows ergogenic benefit in well-nourished athletes. Multis do not increase muscle protein synthesis, VO2 max or strength. |
| mvm-testosterone | Multivitamins raise testosterone. | Unsupported | Zinc and vitamin D correct deficiencies and may restore low levels in deficient men, but there is no evidence that a multivitamin raises testosterone in replete men. |
| wholefood-better | Whole-food vitamins are better absorbed than synthetic vitamins. | Unsupported | A 30-day randomised trial found comparable circulating nutrient changes from organic-sourced and synthetic multis. The actives are the same molecules; whole-food versions cost more and often contain lower mineral doses. |
| d2-equal | Vitamin D2 works as well as D3. | False | At the same dose, D3 raises total 25(OH)D more than D2, and D2 can lower circulating 25(OH)D3. A multivitamin should use D3. |
| fda-approved | Multivitamins are FDA approved. | False | Dietary supplements are not approved by the FDA. They are regulated as a food category under DSHEA, with GMP requirements for manufacturing but no pre-market efficacy or safety review. |
| iron-safe-all | Multivitamins with iron are safe for everyone. | False | Iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children, and adults with haemochromatosis or chronic liver disease can accumulate iron. Prenatal and women's formulas should be kept away from children. |
| mvm-dangerous | Multivitamins are dangerous. | False | Standard-dose multis have an excellent safety record in trials lasting up to 12 years. Specific ingredients at high doses carry real risks: beta-carotene in smokers, high-dose vitamin E, iron in iron-replete adults, zinc without copper, and B6 above 100 mg. |
| mvm-longevity | Multivitamins help you live longer. | False | Nine pooled RCTs show no mortality benefit (OR 0.94, 0.87-1.01), and a 390,124-person cohort found a 4% higher mortality risk among daily users. The claim is not supported by randomised evidence. |
| mvm-replaces-diet | A multivitamin can replace a poor diet. | False | A multivitamin supplies a narrow set of vitamins and minerals. It does not provide protein, fibre, essential fatty acids, energy, or the thousands of phytochemicals in food, and it does not offset the health effects of an unhealthy dietary pattern. |
| stacking-safe | Taking a multivitamin plus individual supplements is always safe. | False | Overlap is the main avoidable risk: a multivitamin plus a D3 capsule plus a B-complex plus zinc can push vitamin D, B6, niacin or zinc toward or past the UL. Add up every source before adding another product. |
| water-soluble-safe | You cannot overdose on water-soluble vitamins. | False | Vitamin B6 above 100 mg/day causes sensory neuropathy, niacin above 35 mg causes flushing and can be hepatotoxic at gram doses, and vitamin C above 2 g causes GI upset and raises oxalate load. |
